Live systems · checked 12 September 2026

“Continuous” survives only if the system survives a miss

A smooth demo can hide a queue, a repeated frame, or a hard scene cut. Ask what continues when the next clip is late, a prompt fails, or a session ends.

Architecture before model

  1. Input: accept prompts, votes, or controls; decide moderation and ordering.
  2. Generation: measure acceptance-to-ready time, not an advertised frame rate.
  3. Buffer: hold enough finished playable seconds for a slow render or retry.
  4. Playout: decide transitions, fallback footage, audio handling, and what black, silence, or a frozen frame means.
  5. Delivery: send to the audience through the chosen browser, WebRTC, RTMP, or platform path.
  6. Recovery: reconnect sessions, preserve the story state if possible, and log the gap.

An uninterrupted broadcast can still reset the story

Project says: Reactor’s FastH3 streaming client can turn chat prompts into a paced RTMP broadcast. It keeps separate generation and playout queues, automatically moves a ready clip into playout, and uses a 24 fps pacer that repeats frames and emits silence when clip-shaped media has a gap.

Project says: the model side has hard clip cuts. It says subject, framing, and voice do not continue into the next independently generated clip, and its tracks go black when nothing is played. Treat “the stream stayed online” and “the story stayed continuous” as two separate tests.

Open session versus buffered clips

Provider says: H3 Max Director uses an open WebRTC session, with new directions sent over a data channel while output tracks play. The provider says later segments retain context and hide a small re-rendered overlap before delivery. Public sessions are finite, so this route still needs a reopening plan.

Project report: Reactor’s FastH3 route can make a ready queue look like a continuous broadcast, but the model does not preserve the world across a clip boundary. Use it for a scheduled or chat-shaped sequence of distinct scenes unless an end-to-end test proves more.

Local server does not mean live system

Project says: FastVideo documents a local FastH3 server route on Apple Silicon MLX and DGX Spark. That gives an operator a local process to reuse across prompts. Its fastest published two-Spark TAEH3 comparison still creates about 5.17 seconds of video in 119 seconds and accepts a preview-quality trade-off.

Use a local FastH3 process to make or prebuild clips. If a viewer’s new input must appear while they wait, record direction-to-ready time, buffer depth, scene boundary, and visible fallback. Those numbers remain Unknown for this pool.

Five questions before you call it live

  • How long until the first playable frame?
  • How long from a new direction to visible change?
  • How many ready seconds sit ahead of playback?
  • What appears after a miss or a failed render?
  • Does the next clip preserve the scene, voice, and viewer’s choice—or begin a new one?

Sources: Reactor FastH3 implementation notes, Reactor streaming-client notes, FastH3 local report, and fal H3 Max Director. This pool has not measured an end-to-end stream.
